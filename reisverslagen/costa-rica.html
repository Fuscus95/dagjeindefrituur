Practical information

🚙 Transport

A regular SUV was sufficient almost everywhere. We hired a Hyundai Tucson for 27 days. Total cost including insurance added locally: €2,572.83. Only a few roads around Caño Negro, Medio Queso and Hotel Rivel were particularly rough.

✈️ Flights

Outbound: Brussels–Newark–San José with United. Our planned return via Montréal to Brussels became Montréal–Frankfurt–Brussels after a delay. Return fare: €710 per person.

🏨 Accommodation

We often booked regular hotels at short notice. Book La Selva, Las Cruces and research stations earlier. Our main bases included Hotel Rivel, Paraiso del Quetzal, Cerro Lodge, Manakin Lodge and La Selva Biological Station.

🗺️ Navigation

We collected stake-outs and GPS points from eBird, Observation.org and trip reports, then exported them to MAPS.ME. We also used offline Google Maps for general navigation.

📱 Mobile coverage

Our Airalo eSIM was easy to use, but coverage was limited in remote birding areas. Offline maps were essential.

☀️ Weather

Mostly pleasant and dry. The lowlands were hot around midday, while the Cerro de la Muerte highlands were noticeably cooler.

1 March 2026 · 68 species

San Ramón

We made an early start from our hotel near the airport and headed straight towards San Ramón for the first serious birding of the trip. The plan was to spend the morning exploring the area around San Luis Adventure Park before making the long afternoon drive east to Hotel Rivel, our base for the Turrialba and Rancho Naturalista region.

We met our local guide, Gustavo “Tavo” Rojas , early in the morning at Camino a la Reserva Alberto Manuel Brenes (CRAMB). Rather than walking the entire road, we slowly worked our way along it by car, stopping regularly to park and bird a few hundred metres on foot before driving a little further and repeating the process. This proved an excellent introduction to Costa Rican birding, and by the end of the day we had recorded 68 species. Among the highlights were Lattice-tailed Trogon (HO), White-throated Shrike-Tanager, Bicolored Antbird, Nightingale Wren and Black-and-yellow Tanager.

The bird that really mattered, however, was Rufous-vented Ground Cuckoo. Later in the morning, Tavo took us to a stake-out on private property near San Luis Adventure Park, where the species had been showing regularly. We were particularly fortunate with our timing: 2026 proved to be an exceptional year for Rufous-vented Ground Cuckoo at this site, while in most years sightings apparently become much less reliable from early March onwards. Securing such a difficult target on the very first day was an excellent start to the trip.

We hired Tavo for the first half-day primarily because this gave us access to the private property where the Rufous-vented Ground Cuckoo was being seen. Before the trip, we had not been able to work out how to arrange access to this private property independently, so hiring Tavo seemed the safest option. Guiding and access cost USD 100 per person.

Birders interested specifically in the cuckoo have a considerably cheaper alternative. We later learned that you can simply drive to the entrance of San Luis Adventure Park and ask whether access to the Rufous-vented Ground Cuckoo stake-out can be arranged. At the time of our visit, this reportedly cost approximately USD 30-35 per person.

One thing we did not fully appreciate on this first visit was the importance of the feeder at San Luis Adventure Park. This is apparently the only known site where Blue-and-gold Tanager regularly visits a feeder, making it one of the easiest and most reliable ways of seeing this otherwise difficult species. Access is straightforward: report to reception and ask to visit the feeder, for which we paid approximately USD 10 per person. We only realised its significance later in the trip and therefore returned to San Ramón later to try for the species.

With Rufous-vented Ground Cuckoo safely on the list before our circuit had properly begun, we left San Ramón in the afternoon and started the long drive east towards Hotel Rivel. This would be our base for exploring the Caribbean foothills around Turrialba and the Rancho Naturalista area the following day.

2 March 2026 · 117 species

Turrialba / Rancho Naturalista region

We had arranged to meet local guide Steven A. Montenegro at first light. Our main target for the morning was Grey-headed Piprites, and Steven took us by 4x4 buggy into a private area called Eco Refugio Los Pavones where the species could be searched for.

Things went remarkably smoothly. The Grey-headed Piprites was secured, and by late morning we had already seen virtually all of our main targets for the area. Tawny-chested Flycatcher (HO) was also found, although the observation remained strictly auditory. The main misses in Steven's area were Yellow-eared Toucanet and Purplish-backed Quail-Dove.

With the morning having gone considerably better than expected, we decided to part ways earlier than planned. Although we had originally booked Steven for a full day, he only charged us for the half-day we had actually spent together: USD 35 per person. We were very happy with his services, and besides knowing the area extremely well, he was simply a great guy to spend a morning birding with. If Grey-headed Piprites is on your target list, he is your Piprites man.

Species diversity had now jumped sharply in the Caribbean foothills, with 117 species recorded around Turrialba during the day.

With our main targets already in the bag, we spent the afternoon birding independently. We first searched around the Platanillo village for Sunbittern, but without success. We then returned to Hotel Rivel - Restaurant & Nature Retreat and spent some time birding around the property. Snowcap was even visiting the hotel garden. Our main miss around Hotel Rivel was Lanceolated Monklet. According to both Steven and the Hotel Rivel staff, the species is quite doable along the river directly in front of the hotel. The staff know the usual spots, so it is definitely worth asking them for recent information on where to look.

A practical note for anyone staying at Hotel Rivel: the final road to the hotel can be rather tricky, particularly on the steeper sections. At the time of our visit, however, considerable work was being done to improve it. The steepest stretches already had concrete wheel tracks - two narrow strips of concrete rather than a fully paved road - which made the climbs considerably easier.

For independent birders, there is in our opinion little reason to stay at Rancho Naturalista itself. Hotel Rivel proved a perfectly good, and considerably more independent, base for birding the Turrialba area. Combined with a morning in the field with Steven Montenegro, it gave us everything we needed to clean up most of our main Caribbean-slope targets without having to build the visit around the famous lodge. For anyone organising Costa Rica independently, the combination of Hotel Rivel + Steven Montenegro is an excellent alternative.

3 March 2026 · 99 species

Turrialba - Ujarrás - Los Quetzales

We started the morning birding along the access road to Hotel Rivel before beginning the drive towards the highlands. This proved well worth the time, with two particularly satisfying sightings: after only hearing Tawny-chested Flycatcher the previous day, we finally managed a proper view, while a Sunbittern made up nicely for our unsuccessful search the previous afternoon.

From Hotel Rivel we continued towards Paraíso, where we stopped at a known stake-out at Ujarrás for Cabanis's Ground Sparrow. The birds proved typically skulky and it took us a while to get a decent view, but with some patience we eventually managed good observations.

Our base for the night was Paraiso del Quetzal Lodge. We mainly chose to stay here because of its convenient location close to Providencia Road, allowing us to easily return there after dark for owling without having to drive a long distance at night.

Almost immediately after arriving, the hummingbird feeders at Paraíso Quetzal Lodge - Km 70 produced some excellent highland species, including Fiery-throated Hummingbird. We then explored the area around the lodge, with Wrenthrush and Black-and-yellow Silky-flycatcher among the highlights.

Note that the main trails are not freely accessible to non-guests and require an entrance fee. Access is through a locked gate, for which a key has to be requested at reception. We were not aware of this at the time and therefore only birded the short sections of publicly accessible trails, the gardens and the area around the feeders. This proved more than sufficient for us, however, as we managed to find all of our target species in these freely accessible areas around the lodge.

One of the day's more unexpected sightings required considerably less effort. While resting on my bed during the afternoon, I suddenly noticed a Buffy-crowned Wood Partridge staring straight at me through the window!

The day was far from over. After dark we headed out along the Providencia Road - PN Los Quetzales, KM 76 for what turned into an extremely successful first night-birding session in the Talamancas. Dusky Nightjar was one of the highlights, but the undisputed star was an Unspotted Saw-whet Owl, which gave us an absolutely superb view. We also recorded Costa Rican Pygmy Owl (HO) and Bare-shanked Screech-Owl (HO), although both remained heard-only.

A good and inexpensive option is Restaurante Los Chespiritos 1, located directly opposite the entrance to Providencia Road (9.616271, -83.819107). It is a simple roadside restaurant serving good, inexpensive food and proved very convenient after a long day of birding.

4 March 2026 · 60 species

Los Quetzales / Cerro de la Muerte

We started the day birding along the Providencia Road, which quickly became one of our favourite places in the highlands. The turn-off from the main road is at (9.615570, -83.818209). The road was wonderfully quiet, with very little traffic and hardly any tourists, making for a particularly enjoyable morning of roadside birding. The main highlights were our first Resplendent Quetzals of the trip and an unexpected bonus in the form of a Silvery-throated Jay. The latter can be very tricky and is much more often missed than seen in recent years!

In the afternoon we drove down the steep and winding road into the San Gerardo de Dota valley. At the very end of the valley, several trails around Sendero Catarata (Waterfall Trail) can be explored freely. Despite arriving well into the afternoon, bird activity was surprisingly good. We soon encountered a large mixed-species feeding flock and spent a very enjoyable session slowly working our way through it as birds moved through the cloud forest around us. The main highlight here was Dark Pewee. The trailhead can be found at (9.546733, -83.811334)

The road down into the San Gerardo de Dota valley is steep, narrow and winding, and getting in and out takes considerably longer than the distance on the map might suggest. It is therefore worth planning your days carefully and avoiding unnecessary trips up and down the valley.

If you want a proper hot dinner at one of the restaurants in the valley, it is advisable to make a reservation in advance. Many places are small and do not necessarily accept walk-ins in the evening. We had dinner at Hotel de Montaña Suria, which we reserved just a few hours beforehand by stopping at the restaurant on our way to the trails. This worked perfectly and gave us a guaranteed dinner after a full day of birding.

5 March 2026 · 60 species

Los Quetzales / Cerro de la Muerte

We spent a second full day in the Talamanca highlands, allowing us to continue cleaning up the regional specialities. The morning was devoted to the trails above Savegre Hotel in the San Gerardo de Dota valley.

From 06:00 onwards, non-guests can pay USD 12 for access to the Hotel Savegre trails. Most visitors take a jeep up to the higher sections of the trail network, which starts around 9.54874, -83.80032. We decided instead to walk up and bird the entire way. This obviously takes considerably longer, but allowed us to work the forest slowly and thoroughly at our own pace.

The morning produced another excellent selection of highland birds. New birds for the trip included, Ochraceous Pewee, Barred Becard and White-winged Tanager. Sulphur-winged Parakeets were conveniently found around the hotel garden.

Higher up the trails we encountered Buffy Tuftedcheek, Spotted Wood-Quail was another frustrating heard-only record. This was one of several occasions during the trip where the final species list looks rather cleaner than the birding actually felt at the time.

In the afternoon we left the valley and headed back towards the highest parts of the Talamancas, spending the remainder of the day birding around the Antenna Road . At this altitude the forest gives way to much more open, windswept habitat, and the species composition changes accordingly. Timberline Wren was one of our main targets here and showed well in the high-elevation scrub.

Volcano Junco proved surprisingly less straightforward. Several trip reports had given us the impression that it would be almost impossible to miss around the antennas, but we still had to search for about an hour before finally finding one. Perhaps we were simply unlucky, but it was certainly not the instant roadside tick we had expected.

In the early evening we made one final attempt for Peg-billed Finch along the Providencia Road. After having missed it earlier, we finally found one - remarkably, at exactly the same coordinates mentioned in Hans Matheve's trip report.

6 March 2026 · 75 species

San Isidro - Talamanca - Peje

After several days in the Talamanca highlands, we started dropping towards the southern Pacific slope. The main aim for the day was to clean up a few specific targets along the way while gradually making our way towards San Vito.

Our first stop was a known stake-out for Turquoise Cotinga around San Isidro. Fortunately, we did not have to wait very long. Shortly after arriving, a male flew into the top of a tall tree, gave us a brief but excellent view and disappeared again after a few minutes.

Apart from the cotinga, however, we found the site itself rather unremarkable and saw little reason to spend much time there once the target was secured.

We then continued to Talamanca Nature Reserve for our first real introduction to the Chiriquí avifauna and some of the hummingbirds of the southern Pacific slope. We paid approximately USD 12 per person for access to the lodge's extensive trail network. There is also a restaurant and a feeding area, making it an easy place to spend several hours.

We first explored part of the trail network, with the undoubted highlight being an excellent sighting of Spotted Wood-Quail. After walking the trails, we spent some time around the gardens and feeders looking for hummingbirds.

Snowy-bellied Hummingbird was a welcome addition, while White-crested Coquette refused to cooperate and remained a target for the following day. Garden Emerald was another new bird for the trip. Red-fronted Parrotlet is occasionally reported from this general area as well, so it is worth keeping an eye out while birding the reserve and surrounding area. In hindsight, we did not see any particularly difficult or essential target species here. Nevertheless, the site fitted our itinerary very well and made for a convenient and enjoyable birding stop during the long transfer eastwards. We would therefore mainly recommend it as a useful stopover rather than as an essential birding destination in its own right.

Our final important stop of the day was near Peje, Buenos Aires, at a known stake-out for Rosy Thrush-Tanager. After sitting out one of the few heavy showers during our trip we managed to connect with the species here before continuing south towards San Vito.

By the end of the day we had reached San Vito, positioning ourselves perfectly for the following day's birding and another dense concentration of southern Costa Rican and Chiriquí specialties.

7 March 2026 · 108 species

San Vito area

We started the morning at a known stake-out for Costa Rican Brushfinch, one of our main targets around San Vito. Fortunately, this one proved straightforward and we connected fairly quickly.

From there we continued to San Joaquín Marsh to look for Chiriqui Yellowthroat. Despite searching the area, we failed to find one. Rather than spending too much of the morning there, we moved on to another nearby road where suitable wetter and more densely vegetated patches looked promising. After some searching, we finally found our Chiriqui Yellowthroat at Calle Mágica.

By around midday we reached Las Cruces Biological Station and the adjoining Wilson Botanical Garden. Birding here was immediately enjoyable and remarkably easy. Even without venturing far onto the trails, there was plenty to see from the observation deck and around the gardens near the restaurant. The flowering vegetation attracted a good selection of hummingbirds, including two important targets: White-crested Coquette, which we had missed the previous day, and White-tailed Emerald.

In the afternoon we headed onto the forest trails with local guide Jeison Figeuroa Sandi. This turned into an excellent session. Jeison knew both the birds and the specific targets extremely well. Among the highlights were Baird's Trogon, Orange-collared Manakin and Black-hooded Antshrike, adding to what was already becoming one of the strongest concentrations of restricted-range species of the trip. Little Tinamou, typically, was also recorded but remained heard-only (HO).

After dinner we made another attempt at night birding around the station, walking some of the trails after dark. This time there was no reward, and the night round produced none of the hoped-for targets.

8 March 2026 · 119 species

Ciudad Neily area - Corcovado

The day started with a short pre-breakfast birding round around Las Cruces Biological Station with Marbled Wood-Quail seen. Chiriqui Quail-Dove also entered the list, although it remained heard-only (HO).

After breakfast we started making our way towards the Osa Peninsula. A stop near Ciudad Neily eventually produced Veraguan Mango in rather unlikely habitat: little more than a row of shrubs in the middle of an agricultural landscape. It took some persistence, but the effort paid off. Giant Cowbird was another new species picked up in the area.

We then continued to a nearby wetland, Coto 47 - Las Pangas, which turned out to be a very enjoyable birding stop. We had only added this stop to the itinerary at the last minute and were not particularly well prepared for it. Plenty of waders and ducks made for relaxed and productive birding. This is also a good place to add Lesser Kiskadee, Rusty-margined Flycatcher and Brown-throated Parakeet to a Costa Rican list, although we did not specifically search for them. Even without these targets, the site was well worth the stop.

Later in the afternoon we checked into our accommodation in the Osa / Corcovado region, which was rather mediocre in terms of value for money. We immediately headed out again to search for Black-cheeked Ant Tanager, with plenty of bird activity continuing into the evening.

After dark we went out for an owling round. A Common Opossum was a nice mammalian bonus, while a Black-and-white Owl gave us an excellent view and became the clear highlight of the night. Our main target, however, the still-undescribed Puntarenas Scops-Owl taxon, unfortunately remained elusive.

9 March 2026 · 117 species

Corcovado

We started early at Rincón Bridge and basically spent the next few hours standing on and around the bridge, simply watching birds. It was a very enjoyable way to bird, although traffic increased considerably later in the morning and made standing on the bridge progressively less pleasant. Most importantly, the hours of scanning paid off. Both Yellow-billed Cotinga and Mangrove Hummingbird showed, securing two of the most important range-restricted targets of the southern Pacific coast.

Later we drove to Dos Brazos, a tiny village on the edge of Corcovado, for some forest birding. Orange-collared Manakin and Black-hooded Antshrike were among the highlights here. Did some pleasant roadside birding just before entering the village.

We stayed at Los Mineros Guesthouse, a very basic place in the village. If you want to have dinner there, definitely arrange this in advance. We had not reserved anything, but fortunately managed to get lunch at the only restaurant in the village. For dinner that evening and breakfast the following morning, we simply worked our way through the provisions we had brought ourselves. Bringing enough food is therefore highly recommended if you want to remain flexible.

A trail leaves from the end of Dos Brazos towards Bolita Rainforest Hostel. Based on our interpretation on site, this route is a public trail, and we simply walked it without reporting to a reception or paying an entrance fee.

10 March 2026 · 91 species

Corcovado - Osa - central Pacific

For our final morning on the Osa Peninsula, we returned to the same trail as the previous day, this time continuing further into the forest. Bird activity was relatively quiet, but the walk still produced Rufous Piha and Green Shrike-Vireo, while Black-cheeked Ant-Tanager provided our second record of the trip. Piping Long-tailed Woodcreeper, however, refused to cooperate and remained a miss.

Afterwards we packed up and started the long drive north towards Carara, turning most of the remainder of the day into a travelling day. We made a short birding stop at a lagoon along the way (GPS: 9.30233, -83.92879). Mangrove Yellow Warbler was also present but frustratingly remained heard-only (HO).

11 March 2026 · 111 species

Carara

We started the day birding the open agricultural areas around Hotel Cerro Lodge. This quickly produced Yellow-naped Amazon and Double-striped Thick-knee before we headed towards Carara National Park.

Carara National Park only opens at 08:00 and an entrance fee of approximately USD 12 per person has to be paid. It is worth arriving around opening time to make sure you can get a ticket and start birding before the trails become busier. At 08:00 it was still relatively quiet during our visit. More groups appeared later in the morning, but despite the number of people on the trails, this never really interfered with our birding.

There is also another trail several kilometres north of the main ticket office. We did not visit this trail, but more information and access details can be found in several other Costa Rica trip reports.

Birding started well almost immediately, with a Tropical Royal Flycatcher close to the parking area. From there we worked our way very slowly through Carara's relatively limited trail network. The forest here is dense and several of our main targets required some patience, but this approach eventually paid off.

Streak-chested Antpitta was one of our main targets and after some searching we managed an excellent view of one bird. Both Stub-tailed Spadebill and Golden-crowned Spadebill were also found. The best sighting of the day came in the afternoon, when we found a Great Tinamou sitting completely motionless in the forest. Easily one of our best views of a tinamou during the trip. Piping Long-tailed Woodcreeper, however, refused to cooperate and remained a miss for the trip, although Wout had already seen the species here on an earlier visit with his family.

Bird activity dropped very quickly during the afternoon, and after leaving Carara we spent some more time birding the agricultural areas around Cerro Lodge. This produced a nice Collared Forest Falcon, while large numbers of Lesser Nighthawks appeared as darkness started to fall.

After dinner we went out once more to look for Pacific Screech Owl. We eventually found one bird and had an excellent view in the flashlight, while a second individual was heard nearby. A very nice way to finish an excellent day around Carara.

12 March 2026 · 92 species

Carara - Orotina - Tárcoles

We started the day by birding the beach and mangroves around Tárcoles. Almost immediately after arriving we unexpectedly found a Rufous-necked Wood Rail, giving us a very easy start to the morning. Later we finally managed a proper view of Mangrove Warbler, after having searched for the species before.

We spent the rest of the morning birding the mangroves and coastal habitat on foot. Repeated attempts for Mangrove Cuckoo remained unsuccessful. Boat trips are also available around Tárcoles and are popular with birding groups, but we preferred exploring independently on foot, which gave us more flexibility to concentrate on our own targets. We even found some time for a quick swim in the Pacific.

On the way back we stopped at the famous Crocodile Bridge for breakfast, coffee and the obligatory look at the American Crocodiles below.

We then spent some time birding around Hotel Cerro Lodge. This quickly produced Olive Sparrow as a new bird for the trip, together with Cinnamon Hummingbird.

In the afternoon we left the wetter habitats around Carara behind and headed towards Orotina to search for species associated with the much drier and more open habitats of the central Pacific lowlands. Temperatures by this point were brutal, but there were still quite a few potential additions to the trip list.

Our main target was Lesser Ground Cuckoo. Before the trip we had collected several recent coordinates from Observation.org and the trip report of Vincent van der Spek. This initially proved rather frustrating, as several of the exact locations no longer appeared to contain suitable habitat.

We therefore changed strategy and started looking ourselves for suitable patches of dry scrub. After finding an area that looked promising, we tried some playback and almost immediately received a response. What followed was a game of patience between us and the cuckoo. After approximately twenty minutes of waiting, we finally managed brief views of two Lesser Ground Cuckoos.

The dry habitat also produced several species that we would not encounter anywhere else during the trip, including Common Ground Dove, Plain-breasted Ground Dove, Banded Wren, Cinnamon-bellied Saltator and Scrub Euphonia. Despite the oppressive afternoon heat, this made the excursion into the dry country extremely worthwhile.

We finished the afternoon by driving to a known stake-out for Blue-vented Hummingbird, followed by another stake-out for Plain-capped Starthroat, connecting with both species. Spot-bellied Bobwhite was another potential speciality of the region, but we had no recent stake-out or concrete plan for the species and did not see one.

Despite the heat, the afternoon produced an excellent selection of species from a habitat type that barely featured anywhere else on our itinerary. For birders mainly interested in cleaning up the dry-country specialities, we felt that spending half a day birding independently around Orotina could potentially make a separate visit to La Ensenada Lodge unnecessary.

13 March 2026 · 90 species

Pacific lowlands - La Ensenada - Monteverde

This was another large transition day, moving from Pacific lowlands through saltpans and up to Monteverde. Ninety species were recorded across a long list of locations.

The Punta Morales saltpans held good numbers of Least Sandpiper, Semipalmated Sandpiper and Western Sandpiper, together with species such as Stilt Sandpiper, Red Knot, Wilson's Plover and Marbled Godwit. Several Black Skimmers added some variety to the large numbers of waders.

The nearby mangroves were also worth checking. Here we finally connected with Prothonotary Warbler, while Panama Flycatcher was another new bird for the trip.

After finishing at the saltpans we started the drive towards Monteverde, leaving the hot and dry Pacific lowlands behind and gradually climbing back into cooler and much greener country. The change in habitat also meant another major change in the bird community.

Once in Monteverde, we headed to Café Colibrí and its hummingbird feeders. This proved almost ridiculously efficient. In roughly the time it took us to drink a coffee, we had already seen all of our main hummingbird targets, including Coppery-headed Emerald, Purple-throated Mountain-gem, Magenta-throated Woodstar and Stripe-tailed Hummingbird.

The feeders are therefore an extremely easy place to see these species, although the surroundings themselves were less enjoyable for birding. There was relatively little activity away from the feeders and the access road was busy with cars.

We spent the final hours of daylight birding within walking distance of our hotel. This produced our first White-eared Ground-sparrows and Long-tailed Manakins, giving us a first taste of the Monteverde cloud-forest avifauna.

Despite the very windy conditions, we decided after dark to make another attempt for Bare-shanked Screech Owl, which until then had remained heard-only on our trip. Against expectations, the attempt actually worked. Around Café Colibrí we found both Mottled Owl and, finally, a Bare-shanked Screech Owl that gave us the visual observation we had been hoping for.

14 March 2026 · 60 species

Monteverde

We started the day at Curi-Cancha Reserve. It is worth arriving well before opening time to make sure you can get a ticket, as the reserve can become quite busy later in the morning.

Overall, bird activity was surprisingly low and we had to work considerably harder for our birds than on most previous days. Nevertheless, the morning produced several good cloud-forest species, including Prong-billed Barbet, Ruddy Woodcreeper and Coppery-headed Emerald.

The undisputed bird of the day was a male Three-wattled Bellbird. We eventually managed an excellent view of one bird, and it turned out to be our only sighting of the species during the entire trip. Definitely one of the highlights of our time in Monteverde.

Ruddy Woodcreeper was another welcome addition and would also remain our only record of the species during the trip.

By the afternoon bird activity had dropped even further. We stopped for a coffee and spent some more time birding around Monteverde, but added very little for the remainder of the day.

Interestingly, Wout had visited the same area earlier with his family and had experienced considerably better bird activity at Curi-Cancha, so our relatively quiet morning may simply have been down to the conditions on the day.

15 March 2026 · 71 species

Monteverde

After the relatively quiet birding at Curi-Cancha the previous day, this turned into one of our best days in Monteverde. We would strongly recommend the hike to San Gerardo Research Station to anyone birding the area. The trail passes through beautiful cloud forest, was extremely quiet during our visit and had plenty of bird activity throughout the day.

We had arranged access and lunch at the research station in advance. The hike starts from the parking area at Santa Elena Cloud Forest Reserve. We were perhaps a little too polite and waited until 07:00 for the ticket office to open so we could report our presence. Santa Elena is a separate reserve, which we did not visit, and we later discovered that checking in at its reception was not actually necessary for the hike to San Gerardo.

Even while waiting around the parking area, birding started extremely well. We found Black-breasted Wood-Quail and Golden-bellied Flycatcher before we had even properly started walking.

From there we slowly birded the approximately 5 km trail towards San Gerardo Research Station. The walk passes through excellent cloud forest and was remarkably peaceful. During the entire hike we encountered only one other group of people, a major contrast with some of the busier reserves around Monteverde.

Bird activity was excellent. Among the highlights were Keel-billed Motmot, Silvery-fronted Tapaculo, Slaty-backed Nightingale-thrush, Streak-breasted Treehunter, White-bellied Mountain-gem and Middle American Leaftosser. Black-headed Antthrush also gave us an excellent view. Western Woodhaunter was less cooperative and remained heard-only. It was particularly satisfying to finally get a proper view of Silvery-fronted Tapaculo.

We had pre-booked lunch at San Gerardo Research Station and ate there before slowly making our way back towards Santa Elena. We eventually reached the car again at around 17:30, tired but very satisfied after an extremely productive full day in the field.

We did not stay overnight at the research station, but in hindsight this is something we would seriously consider. Our recommendation would be to book an extra night at Manakin Lodge, leave most of your luggage there, and take only what you need for the night at San Gerardo. This allows you to leave a virtually empty car at the trailhead parking area rather than having all your luggage sitting unattended in the car for the entire day. More importantly, an overnight stay would give you considerably more time to bird this excellent and remarkably quiet area. .

Even without staying overnight, the hike to San Gerardo Research Station was one of our favourite birding experiences around Monteverde and one we would strongly recommend to independent birders.

Red-fronted Parrotlet is occasionally recorded here as well, but we missed it. It remains a notoriously difficult species to find. Most birders who managed to see it that year did so at night, using a thermal scope at a known roost site.

16 March 2026 · 108 species

Monteverde - Caño Negro

We spent the first few hours of the morning doing some final birding around the SGBS trail at Monteverde, picking up another Three-wattled Bellbird and Black-breasted Wood-Quail.

Afterwards, we started the long drive north, descending from the highlands and eventually reaching the wetlands around Caño Negro. After checking into Hotel de Campo, we first spent some time birding around the hotel grounds. A Jabiru flying overhead was a particularly nice welcome to the area.

For the final few hours of daylight, we headed to the boat landing and simply stayed there watching whatever came past. It was a pleasant and relaxed introduction to the Caño Negro wetlands. Nicaraguan Grackle was one of our targets here, but unfortunately remained a miss.

The owner of Hotel de Campo is a keen nature photographer and knows the local wildlife well, so he can be a useful source of information for some of the area's specialties.

17 March 2026 · 137 species

Caño Negro

We spent the entire day birding with Chambita, doing two separate boat trips: one around Caño Negro in the morning and a second afternoon trip at Medio Queso, much closer to the Nicaraguan border. With 137 species, this was the statistical high point of the trip and certainly one of its ornithological highlights as well.

The morning boat trip focused on Sungrebe, Boat-billed Heron, Snowy Cotinga and Olive-crowned Yellowthroat. It turned into an excellent session, with Sungrebe recorded no fewer than five times. Boat-billed Heron and Snowy Cotinga added further quality to an already very productive morning.

In the afternoon we drove towards Medio Queso for our second boat trip. On the way there, we already managed to pull in Nicaraguan Seed-Finch, one of the important regional targets. The afternoon boat trip had a much stronger focus on the difficult marsh specialties close to the Nicaraguan border: Least Bittern, Pinnated Bittern, Yellow-breasted Crake and Nicaraguan Grackle. All targets eventually showed, meaning that together with the Seed-Finch we had secured the main obvious restricted-range wetland targets we had hoped for.

Chambita proved to be an excellent guide with an impressive knowledge of the area. He asked for our target list beforehand and tailored the day accordingly. For many of these wetland specialties, having both a knowledgeable local guide and access by boat makes an enormous difference; without a boat, your chances of connecting with several of the main targets are probably very limited.

On the drive back we managed to add another classic species to the Costa Rica trip list: a flat tyre. Simon replaced it in record time, benefiting from extensive previous training in Armenia, Morocco, Panama and Thailand. Apparently changing tyres is becoming an increasingly established part of our international birding skill set.

The day still had one final birding surprise. In the fading light on the way back, we had a very nice Great Potoo and Striped Owl, a fitting end to an exceptional day.

We finished with a cheap dinner at a small local restaurant in the village, after what had easily been one of the best birding days of the trip.

18 March 2026 · 74 species

Volcán Tenorio

This was mainly a transition day, taking us south again through Volcán Tenorio, before continuing towards Arenal in the afternoon.

We started the morning by trying a public trail in the foothills, primarily in search of Black-eared Wood-Quail. The quail refused to cooperate, but we were more than compensated with excellent views of an Ornate Hawk-Eagle.

Later we continued to Heliconias Lodge, where we paid the entrance fee and spent some time exploring their trail system. This proved more productive, with Tody Motmot and Crested Owl as the main highlights.

In hindsight, a better strategy for the quail might have been to start at Heliconias Lodge early in the morning, rather than visiting later in the day.

Afterwards, we continued the drive towards Arenal, bringing the northern wetland part of the trip to an end and setting ourselves up for the next section.

19 March 2026 · 98 species

Arenal

We spent the entire day birding the extensive trail network and gardens of Arenal Observatory Lodge, paying the day entrance fee and essentially roaming the property from morning until late afternoon. Between the forest trails, gardens, feeders and the excellent observation deck, there was more than enough to keep us occupied for a full day.

The change from the open wetlands of the north back to rich Caribbean foothill forest was immediately noticeable. We recorded 98 species, with Black-crested Coquette and Ocellated Antbird among the most important additions. We once again spent time searching for Yellow-eared Toucanet, one of our remaining targets, but despite the hours in suitable habitat it continued to avoid us.

For independent birders, there is no real need to stay at Arenal Observatory Lodge itself. Day visitors can enter early, pay the entrance fee and freely explore the extensive trail network, gardens, feeders and observation deck. For us, spending the full day here while staying elsewhere worked perfectly well.

20 March 2026 · 76 species

Arenal

We started our second full day around Arenal along Peninsula Road, a public road beside the lake, with Thicket Antpitta as the main target. The birds were around, but getting everyone onto one proved considerably harder. It took at least an hour of patient searching before everyone finally managed a proper view.

Afterwards, we returned to Arenal Observatory Lodge, where we once again spent the rest of the day roaming the extensive trails and gardens. Bronze-tailed Plumeleteer was the main new addition, while Black-crested Coquette made another appearance.

By this stage, almost three weeks into the trip, the common species were no longer driving the list. New additions increasingly required specific searches for local, scarce or secretive birds, and this day illustrated that perfectly: fewer new species, but considerably more effort for each one.

21 March 2026 · 74 species

San Carlos - San Ramón

We left Arenal early and headed back towards the Alberto Manuel Brenes Reserve (CRAMB) near San Ramón, returning to the area where our Costa Rica trip had started almost three weeks earlier. It was a pleasant return to familiar ground, although this time with a considerably longer bird list behind us.

There had been very recent eBird reports of Bare-necked Umbrellabird, giving us a good reason to make another attempt. Unfortunately, the umbrellabird remained elusive. The morning was far from wasted, searching for the very range restricted Rufous-browed Tyrannulet.

Another important target here was Uniform Crake. One bird eventually responded to playback from dense vegetation, but stubbornly remained heard-only (HO).

Later we continued to San Luis Adventure Park, where things started considerably faster. Within about ten seconds of arriving at the feeders, we were looking at Blue-and-gold Tanagers. After spending so much of the later part of the trip searching specifically for difficult and localized species, it was refreshing to have an important target fall quite this easily. Bronze-tailed Plumeleteer was also present around the feeders.10USD pp entrance fee

We then headed onto the trails, which proved very productive. The real highlight was finally getting proper views of Purplish-backed Quail-Dove. A friendly local birder/employee helped us locate the birds and made sure we got onto them. We gave him a small tip afterwards, although he seemed to be helping us mainly for the love of the game!

22 March 2026 · 71 species

San Ramón - Poás - central highlands

We started the morning with yet another attempt for Bare-necked Umbrellabird at CRAMB, but once again without success. By this stage of the trip it was becoming one of those species that simply refused to cooperate.

Simon had missed White-bellied Mountain-gem earlier in Monteverde and, after checking recent eBird observations, found a promising stake-out at Bosque Nuboso, a small café with a hummingbird garden in the area. After a bit of waiting at the feeders, White-bellied Mountain-gem showed nicely. According to the owner, Bare-necked Umbrellabird occasionally visits the property to feed on fruit as well, but unsurprisingly it did not do us that favour.

We then started the longer drive through the central highlands towards Volcán Poás. By the time we arrived in the Poás area, however, the weather had deteriorated considerably. Rain effectively ended the birding for the day, and we did not see anything of note after arriving.

This was primarily a positioning and clean-up day before heading back towards the Caribbean lowlands. With the trip list already well established, there were few easy additions left: almost every remaining gap now required a specific location, stake-out or targeted search.

23 March 2026 · 82 species

Central highlands - Sarapiquí - La Selva

We left the Poás area and started descending towards the Caribbean lowlands, with several targeted stops along the way before continuing to La Selva.

Our first major stop was La Paz Waterfall Gardens. Unfortunately, access requires paying the rather steep entrance fee for the entire attraction, while our interest was almost exclusively in the hummingbird feeders. They did, however, eventually deliver the main target: Black-bellied Hummingbird, the only new species of the day. The feeders mentioned near the entrance in Rob Jansen's trip report were no longer present during our visit. It may therefore be worth carefully checking recent eBird observations for a cheaper alternative if Black-bellied Hummingbird is your only reason for stopping here. On the positive side, La Paz lies conveniently along the route towards La Selva.

We then made another targeted stop at the parking area of La Paz Waterfall, where recent information suggested a possible stake-out for Green-fronted Lancebill. No luck for us. A much more relaxed stop followed at this soda restaurant, which had some very nice feeders. These provided particularly good photographic opportunities for Emerald Toucanet and made it a worthwhile break on the drive east.

From there we continued down the Caribbean slope towards Sarapiquí and finally arrived at La Selva Biological Station, where we would stay for the next three nights. Great Green Macaw was recorded almost immediately after arrival, providing an excellent introduction to what would be the final major block of lowland rainforest birding of the trip. Rob Jansen’s trip report covers all the trails extensively and is an excellent reference for planning your visit.

La Selva Biological Station is operated by the Organization for Tropical Studies (OTS), the same organisation that manages Las Cruces Biological Station. As at Las Cruces, we arranged our stay directly with OTS by email, which is probably the easiest way to organise accommodation.

We paid approximately US$100 per person per night. This again included accommodation, all meals, coffee and other basic refreshments, as well as one guided walk during our stay. Although not particularly cheap, the package was very convenient for birders, as you are staying directly inside one of Costa Rica's most important lowland rainforest reserves and can spend practically all your time birding.

24 March 2026 · 86 species

La Selva / Sarapiquí

We spent the entire day birding around La Selva Biological Station, eventually recording 86 species. Compared with what we had expected from such a famous lowland rainforest site, however, bird activity was surprisingly slow and we had to work hard for our birds, particularly during the morning.

In the afternoon we joined the guided walk included with our stay. Birding remained fairly quiet, but the guide showed us a roost of Honduran White Bats, which was a very nice non-avian highlight of the day.

One of our main targets at La Selva was Agami Heron, but despite searching the appropriate areas, we failed to find one. Overall, a pleasant day in an impressive reserve, although considerably harder work bird-wise than we had expected.

25 March 2026 · 50 species

La Selva

Our second full day at La Selva Biological Station was mainly devoted to cleaning up the remaining targets. With the trip list already becoming saturated, the overall species total mattered much less by this stage: we were now working hard for a handful of very specific birds.

Slaty-breasted Tinamou was also heard but remained frustratingly out of sight. Eventually, all the effort produced White-fronted Nunbird as a new species for the trip. Rather ironically, Blue-chested Hummingbird was added only after a full day of hard work on the trails, when one simply appeared right in front of our accommodation.

There was plenty of non-avian entertainment as well. A Fer-de-lance was an impressive encounter along the trails. An Ocelot latrine was located near 10.437266, -84.000323, and a camera trap there had apparently recorded an Ocelot every day during our stay. We spent some time waiting nearby and scanning with the thermal scope, but unfortunately never saw it ourselves.

After dark, patience produced better results. We found a Mexican Hairy Porcupine and eventually a Kinkajou. For the latter, it is worth staying up late and repeatedly scanning the suspension bridge over the river. Michael did exactly that and was rewarded with an excellent observation. We also saw both Brown-throated Three-toed Sloth and Hoffmann’s Two-toed Sloth and Collared Peccary was common during our stay at La Selva. Strawberry Poison Frog and Green-and-black Poison Dart Frog were further nice non-avian encounters.

26 March 2026 · 54 species

La Selva - Central Valley

We had deliberately kept this day as a back-up day in the itinerary and eventually decided to spend one more morning at La Selva Biological Station. Despite several days of birding here already, there were still a few additions to be found. As on the previous days, however, we had to work hard for them.

We spent most of the day walking the trails, with Olive-backed Quail-Dove among our main targets. We eventually heard one around STR 500/550, but despite our efforts it stubbornly remained heard-only. The final morning produced Pied Puffbird, Acadian Flycatcher and Central American Pygmy-Owl. A male Snowy Cotinga flying over the trail was another excellent sighting and a particularly fitting Caribbean-lowland highlight.

In the afternoon we finally left La Selva and headed back into the highlands. Along the way, we made another attempt for Green-fronted Lancebill, this time at a different stake-out< The location did not look particularly promising at first: little more than a small stream beside a busy road. The species apparently regularly perches on the rocks here, however, and this time the gamble paid off. We found the Green-fronted Lancebill surprisingly quickly, making up for our earlier failed attempt near La Paz.

27 March 2026 · 32 species

Casa Dowii - San José

There was still time for one final day of birding before heading towards the airport. We spent most of it at the feeders of Casa Dowii, with Buff-fronted Quail-Dove as the main target. After 27 days and hundreds of species, it would have been a very welcome final addition to the trip list. Unfortunately, despite spending hours waiting and watching the feeders, it never appeared. A rather painful final miss.

With birding finally finished, there was only one important target left: a car wash. After 27 days of Costa Rican roads, the rental car had arguably earned its own trip report. We then headed towards the airport area, bringing an exceptional month of birding around Costa Rica to an end.

Targets, endemics & notable misses

Mainland endemics

We recorded Coppery-headed Emerald, Mangrove Hummingbird and Cabanis's Ground Sparrow, along with the Gray-tailed/White-throated Mountain-gem taxonomic complex. We missed none of the four mainland-endemic benchmarks used in the original report.

The painful miss

Bare-necked Umbrellabird eluded us despite repeated attempts during the main circuit. Wout had seen it at Centro Manú before the main trip, which made the miss sting even more.

Other misses

Black-eared Wood-Quail, Lovely Cotinga, Yellow-eared Toucanet, Red-fronted Parrotlet, Piping Long-tailed Woodcreeper and Buff-fronted Quail-Dove also remained on our wish list.
